# Science Video Topic Selection

Return a research-backed editorial decision, not a list of clever titles. An accessible question and an easy shoot are not evidence of originality. Familiar subjects are welcome if this take shows or explains something the nearest existing work does not. Novelty and audience appeal are separate: neither can substitute for the other.

**If the user has already commissioned a specific question**, preserve it and apply only the relevant science, feasibility and treatment checks below. Do not require rival topics, a novelty contest, or a new selection decision.

## 1. Map what already exists

For an unassigned editorial brief in this series, anchor discovery to `editorial_positioning` in [series profile](../../config/series-profile.json) so episodes build a recognizable channel identity rather than scattering into unrelated trivia:

- **Primary lane (~80%) — everyday objects and visible mechanisms:** everyday objects, packaging, household and daily devices, food and kitchen physics, common materials, and everyday safety or mechanical structures that an ordinary adult recognizes in the very first frame without background preamble.
- **Secondary lane (~20%) — observable physical or scale paradoxes:** immediately recognizable natural or physical phenomena framed around a stark concrete contradiction or physical limit (apparent impossibility of mass, height, pressure, force, phase or scale) that reads visually on screen.
- **Deprioritized for default editorial picks (use only when explicitly user-commissioned or anchored to a clear everyday/engineering mechanism transfer):** standalone animal trivia or single-species oddities, and abstract, invisible multi-layer electronic, coding, or clinical-imaging tutorials that cannot establish a concrete visible physical contradiction within the first two seconds.

Category ratios are editorial guidance, not hard quotas. Treat a misconception, reveal, cutaway, experiment, or before/after comparison as a treatment, not a category.

- Identify the audience and what they already know. Form several genuinely different **questions**, with at least one viable rival; keep them provisional until researched. A new title for the same question, answer, and demonstration is not an alternative.
- For each serious contender, search beyond the exact proposed title: use the everyday question and its variants, the formal mechanism or experiment name, and the key shot or comparison. Include other languages where useful. Check the target platform's authorized public surfaces, relevant public sources elsewhere, and available prior work from the series. Search your own past work by what it answered and showed, not just its title.
- Inspect the closest competing works at the relevant scene or substantive passage. Record the link or work ID, inspection scope (timestamp, transcript passage, or equivalent), what each actually asks, answers, and shows, and which is closest. A title, search result, or an inaccessible player cannot establish what a video omitted. Record queries, date, coverage, and access gaps; repeated queries with no close hit do not establish a first-ever claim. If a missing comparison could plausibly invalidate the proposed difference, mark it pending.
- Treat recurring audience questions, relevant search activity, or substantive comments as demand clues, not proof that a particular take will perform. Separate actual observations from editorial inference. Missing data is unknown; do not invent popularity, viewers, or thresholds.

## 2. Verify the appeal, difference, and evidence

- Test the topic before polishing the title. In plain words, what would make a nonspecialist want the answer, and what specific moment would be worth watching? Prefer **contradiction-first questions** ("it looks or behaves like A, yet B happens — why/how?") over flat encyclopedia prompts ("how does X work?"). Name the source of interest: for example, a concrete structural/physical contradiction, a surprising everyday observation, a meaningful everyday choice, a result viewers can predict for themselves, or an arresting hidden mechanism they rarely get to see. If it feels like homework without hype, hiding the subject, or a dramatic edit, reframe or drop it. This is editorial judgment unless actual audience evidence exists.
- Check **single-mechanism scope tightness** for standalone feed episodes: verify that one clear core mechanism resolves the episode cleanly and visibly without sprawling into a multi-subtopic encyclopedia tour. When an everyday object hides several distinct mechanisms, isolate the single sharpest visible contradiction for one focused film rather than bundling tangents. Let explanation clarity determine duration, with no mandatory length floor or ceiling.
- Write the difference against the **closest actual comparison**: the question it leaves unresolved, the boundary or tradeoff it misses, or the evidence this episode can make visible in a way that materially changes what the viewer understands. Name the expected new observation, and what the viewer can distinguish or predict afterward. If swapping our title onto the comparison would still describe the same story and demonstration, revise or reject.
- Open and read appropriate primary evidence: original work, official technical documentation, or a directly relevant authoritative explanation. Record the claim supported, the specific passage/figure and its conditions, and what the source cannot establish. If sources disagree on the central mechanism or no suitable primary support is available, keep it pending. A paper can support the science without giving permission to use its media.
- Specify a credible route to the key shot and verify **visual-demonstration fit**: what we would capture or build, what changes and stays fixed, what result is observable, and what would disprove the proposed explanation. Favor questions whose decisive proof is a visible state change, cutaway, slow-motion failure, force/flow path, or scale contrast that the planned self-created medium can show faithfully; do not select a topic whose credibility depends on dense, error-prone precision assemblies or invisible mathematical layers unless a controlled 3D (`Blender`/`vork`) route is explicitly chosen to model them accurately. For descriptive topics name the structure, sequence, or relationship that the shot must reveal instead of forcing a controlled experiment. Distinguish actual observation from illustration/simulation and reference-only imagery from production-eligible assets. No script, rendered proof, or finished media is required to decide, but an unavailable defining shot cannot be promised.

## 3. Decide and hand off

For editorial selection, compare the strongest researched options at equal depth. Use plain language and these decisions, without a numerical score or forced winner:

- **Select** only when the question gives the intended viewer a concrete reason to care and the comparison, specific difference, primary evidence and feasible visible payoff all hold up. Explain why it beats the strongest feasible rival.
- **Revise / pending** if a sharper question might work, a relevant comparison could not be inspected, or science or feasibility remains unresolved. State the check that would resolve it; keep researching while practical. Do not send provisional candidates as recommendations.
- **Reject** when the closest work already delivers the same question, answer and evidence, or this version has no substantive payoff. A classic demonstration with no new explanatory value, merely better aesthetics, or a trending title alone is not enough.
- **User-commissioned topic:** preserve the requested topic and answer scope. Surface known relevant overlap and improve the treatment within scope rather than rejecting or replacing the commission for lack of novelty. Flag unresolved factual, rights, or feasibility problems; don't invent proof or approval.

Return a compact decision card: audience, question, and the concrete reason to watch; one-sentence answer; closest comparisons and checked scope; what's materially new; primary evidence and limits; visible proof and feasible production route; decision, any remaining gap, and for editorial selection the strongest rival and why. Keep evidence private where appropriate. If handing off to a production workflow, map the result into its existing topic-selection record; don't add another approval stage. Selection does not authorize rendering, platform actions, or publishing.
