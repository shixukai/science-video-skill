Within the paper's wettable, porous experimental surfaces that retain their structure, liquid absorption can supply pore gas to bubbles while droplet height also falls. After a bubble reaches the air–liquid interface, it bursts and produces a liquid jet, which then fragments into small droplets. Repeated-impact experiments indicate that a thin gas film trapped at the interface cannot alone explain the phenomenon; gas supplied from pores is important. This is the authors' experimental explanation, not an independent validation by this round's screenshots.

That body text does not replace further checks of specific imaging conditions, film-opening geometry, jet-neck timing, or numerical parameters. It does not establish that every raindrop behaves this way or prove odor components, dose, or perceived intensity. Do not shrink an air bubble and turn it directly into a water drop, or treat “bubble bursting” and “liquid-jet breakup” as one object-change event.
